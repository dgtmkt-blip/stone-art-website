Solid stone slab is beautiful, but it does not bend. On a curved wall, a column or an arch, slab has to be cut into small pieces that then line up unevenly. Flexible stone veneer solves that problem by wrapping the whole shape in a single sheet of real stone.

This is one of the clearest reasons architects and interior designers choose veneer. Below is how it works and where it opens up new design options.

## How a thin sheet bends

The visible face of flexible stone veneer is real stone, only about 1.5 to 2 mm thick. It is bonded to a flexible backing of resin and fibreglass. That backing holds the stone together and lets the whole sheet bend around a shape without cracking.

Industry sources describe sheets of 1 to 3 mm, weighing about 2 kg per square metre, bending around columns, arches, furniture and curved features. Stoneart's own [Natural Stone sheets](/products/natural-stone) are 1.5 to 2 mm thick and come in sizes up to 1220 × 2440 mm, so a single sheet can wrap a wide column or follow a long curve with few joints.

How tight a curve a particular sheet can take depends on the stone and the backing. Confirm the minimum bend radius with your supplier before you specify.

## Where it changes a design

- **Columns and pillars.** Wrap an existing structural column in stone without heavy jacketing. A single sheet can cover the whole surface.
- **Curved walls.** A long curved reception wall, a lobby or a rounded corridor becomes one continuous stone surface instead of a patchwork of panels.
- **Arches and vaults.** The soffit of an arch or the inside of a vault can carry stone that would otherwise be impossible.
- **Rounded bar fronts and reception desks.** Furniture that curves can read as a solid piece of stone.
- **Spiral staircase walls.** The curve of a stairwell is one of the hardest shapes to clad; thin veneer handles it in sections.
- **Niches and alcoves.** Small, rounded shapes inside walls get the same stone finish as the flat sections around them.

## Design choices that work well with curves

- **Pick a stone with movement.** Veined marble and layered slate look most natural when they curve, because the pattern follows the shape. Flat, uniform stones can look painted when curved.
- **Mind the joints.** On a long curve, place joints where they are least visible, usually at the ends of the shape or where another material meets it.
- **Light the shape.** A grazing light down a curved stone wall emphasises the texture and the curve at the same time.
- **Think about the finish at the top and bottom.** Edges on curved surfaces are harder than on flat walls; use trim or a reveal to keep the detail clean.

## Installation notes

For curves, the installer typically:

1. Prepares the curved surface, so it is clean, sound and smooth.
2. Dry-fits the sheet to confirm it will follow the curve without stress.
3. Uses a flexible adhesive the supplier recommends.
4. Presses the sheet from the centre out towards the edges, so no air is trapped.
5. Finishes the edges carefully, as these are more visible on a curved wall than on a flat one.

## Common questions

### Can veneer bend around a column of any size?

Within reason, yes. Very small columns need a thinner sheet or a stone that bends more easily. Ask your supplier for the minimum column diameter they recommend.

### Does the stone crack on tight curves?

Not if the right stone and the right radius are chosen. Softer stones and thinner sheets handle tighter curves. Harder stones and thicker sheets handle gentler curves.

### Will the joints show on a curve?

Joints on curves are a little more visible than on flat walls, because the eye can see light pick up the edge. Butt the joints tightly, align the pattern, and most people will not notice them.
